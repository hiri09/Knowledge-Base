# Valuation — price vs. value

**Price** is what the last two people agreed on. **Value** is the cash the business
will hand its owners over its life, discounted back to today. They're related the way
weather is related to climate.

## The core idea (DCF, without the spreadsheet)

A business is worth all its future free cash flow, with future rupees discounted
because (a) you could've earned a safe return instead and (b) the future is uncertain.

## Simple example

A shop reliably throws off ₹1,00,000 a year forever, and a safe FD pays 7%.
Value ≈ 1,00,000 ÷ 0.07 = **₹14,28,571**.

Now the same shop, but growing 3% a year forever:
Value ≈ 1,00,000 ÷ (0.07 − 0.03) = **₹25,00,000**.

Three percent of growth nearly doubled the value. **That's why growth stocks carry
high multiples — and why a small cut to expected growth destroys so much price.**
Same math, in reverse: if rates rise from 7% to 9%, the no-growth shop is worth
₹11.1 lakh. The shop didn't change. The alternative did.

## Practical versions

- **Relative**: compare P/E, EV/EBITDA against the same company's history and its
  direct peers. Fast, and only as good as the comparison.
- **Earnings yield**: EPS ÷ price, the inverse of P/E. Compare it against the 10-year
  government bond yield. If a risky business yields less than a risk-free bond, you
  are being paid nothing for the risk.

## How I'd get this wrong

Believing a DCF because it has decimals. Change growth by 2% and the answer moves
50%. A valuation is a way to make assumptions explicit, not a price target.
