# The three statements (and which one lies least)

Every company publishes three. They answer three different questions.

**1. P&L (income statement) — did it earn?** Over a period.
Revenue − costs = profit. Contains judgement calls (depreciation, provisions,
revenue recognition), so it's the easiest to flatter.

**2. Balance sheet — what does it own and owe?** At one instant.
Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Always. Tells you about debt, inventory, receivables.

**3. Cash flow — did money actually arrive?** Over a period.
Split into Operating (the business), Investing (buying/selling assets), Financing
(loans, equity, dividends). **Cash is a fact; profit is an opinion.**

## Simple example

A company sells ₹100 cr of goods on credit, collects nothing yet.
- P&L: revenue ₹100 cr, looks like a great year.
- Balance sheet: receivables +₹100 cr (an IOU, not money).
- Cash flow from operations: ₹0.

Profit up, cash flat, receivables ballooning, two years running = the single most
common fraud/stress pattern there is.

## The 60-second read

1. Is **operating cash flow** roughly tracking **net profit** over 3-5 years?
2. Is debt rising faster than profit?
3. Are receivables/inventory growing faster than revenue?
4. Is the company funding dividends out of borrowings?

Any "no" is not a verdict — it's a question to go answer.

## How I'd get this wrong

Reading only the headline PAT number in a news app. The number I'd want is multi-year
operating cash flow, and it never appears in a headline.
