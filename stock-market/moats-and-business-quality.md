# Moats — why some businesses keep earning

High profits attract competitors, and competition destroys profits. A **moat** is the
reason that doesn't happen to a particular company.

The real ones:

1. **Switching costs** — leaving is painful. (A bank's core software; an ERP.)
2. **Network effects** — each new user makes it better for everyone. (An exchange,
   a marketplace, UPI apps.)
3. **Cost advantage** — structurally cheaper production: scale, location, process.
   (A cement plant next to its own limestone mine.)
4. **Intangibles** — brand you'll pay more for, patents, a licence others can't get.
5. **Efficient scale** — the market is only big enough for one or two players, so
   nobody rational enters. (A gas pipeline to one city.)

Not moats: being first, "great management", a good product, a big ad budget.
All copyable.

## Simple example

Two paint companies. One has 70,000 dealers, mixing machines installed in each, and
a brand customers ask for by name. The other has a better formula and a lower price.
The second one still loses, because paint is bought from whoever the dealer stocks.
Distribution was the moat, not chemistry.

## How you see it in the numbers

A moat shows up as **ROCE staying high (say >18%) for 7-10 years** while revenue
grows. No moat = ROCE drifts toward the cost of capital as rivals arrive.

## How I'd get this wrong

Confusing a great product with a great business. The product I love may be sold by a
company with no pricing power, thin margins and three identical competitors.
