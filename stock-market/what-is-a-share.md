# What a share actually is

A share is a **slice of ownership in a business**. Not a betting slip, not a number
that wiggles. If a company is cut into 1,00,000 shares and you hold 100, you own
0.1% of everything it owns and 0.1% of every rupee it earns.

## Simple example

Ravi's tea stall is worth ₹10,00,000 and earns ₹2,00,000 a year.
He splits it into 1,000 shares → each share = ₹1,000 of stall, ₹200 of yearly profit.
You buy 10 shares for ₹10,000. You now own 1% of the stall and have a claim on
₹2,000 of its yearly profit — paid out as **dividend**, or kept inside the business
to buy a second stall (which makes your share worth more later).

## Why a company sells shares at all

To raise money it never has to repay. A loan must be paid back with interest; equity
is sold once, forever. In exchange, the owner gives up a slice of future profits and
some control.

## The two ways you make money

1. **Dividend** — your cut of profit, paid in cash.
2. **Capital gain** — the business grows, so the slice is worth more than you paid.

Over a long horizon, #2 does most of the work, and it only happens if the underlying
business earns more over time. Price follows earnings eventually; it just takes
detours that can last years.

## How I'd get this wrong

Treating the ticker as the thing. The ticker is a *price for* the thing. If I can't
say in one sentence what the business sells and who pays for it, I'm not investing,
I'm gambling on a symbol.
