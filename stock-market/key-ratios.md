# Key ratios — what each one actually asks

A ratio is a question in compressed form. Learn the question, not the number.

| Ratio | Formula | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| **P/E** | Price ÷ EPS | How many years of current earnings am I paying? |
| **P/B** | Price ÷ book value | What am I paying per ₹1 of net assets? |
| **ROE** | Net profit ÷ equity | How well does it turn owners' money into profit? |
| **ROCE** | EBIT ÷ (debt + equity) | Same, but including borrowed money — harder to fake |
| **Debt/Equity** | Total debt ÷ equity | How much of this is borrowed and fragile? |
| **Operating margin** | Operating profit ÷ revenue | How much of each ₹100 of sales survives? |
| **Interest coverage** | EBIT ÷ interest | Can it even pay the interest? Below ~3 = watch |

## Simple example

Stock at ₹500, EPS ₹25 → P/E = 20. You're paying ₹20 for ₹1 of annual earnings; at
flat earnings it takes 20 years to earn your money back. P/E 60 means the market
expects earnings to grow a lot — if they don't, the multiple collapses even when the
business is fine.

## What each one hides

- **P/E** is meaningless for loss-makers and cyclicals (a commodity stock looks
  *cheapest* at peak profits, right before the cycle turns).
- **ROE** can be inflated purely by taking on debt. Always read it next to D/E, or
  use ROCE.
- **P/B** matters for banks, barely matters for an asset-light software firm.
- All of them are backward-looking. The price is forward-looking.

## How I'd get this wrong

Screening for "P/E under 10" and calling it value. Cheap ratios usually mean the
market expects earnings to fall. The job is to figure out whether it's right.
