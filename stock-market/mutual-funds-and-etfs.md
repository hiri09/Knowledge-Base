# Mutual funds, index funds and ETFs

A fund pools money from many people and buys a basket. You own **units** of the
basket, priced at **NAV** (net asset value per unit).

- **Active fund** — a manager picks stocks, tries to beat the index. Expense ratio
  ~1-2%/yr. Most don't beat it after costs, and the ones that did last decade are
  not reliably the ones that will next decade.
- **Index fund** — mechanically holds the index. Expense ratio ~0.1-0.2%/yr.
- **ETF** — an index fund that trades on the exchange like a share. Same idea,
  intraday price, needs a demat account, and can trade slightly off its real NAV
  if volume is thin.

## Why the expense ratio decides so much

₹10,00,000 compounding at 12% for 20 years:
- at 0.2% fee → ₹93.1 lakh
- at 1.5% fee → ₹73.4 lakh

**₹19.7 lakh — about 21% of the outcome — paid for the privilege of being managed.**
The fee is charged whether the fund wins or loses.

## SIP

A fixed amount, monthly, automatically. It does two things: it removes the timing
decision (the one you're worst at), and it buys more units when prices are low. It
is not magic — in a market that falls for a decade, an SIP loses money too — but it
beats the real alternative, which is waiting for a "good time" and never entering.

## Where this fits for a beginner

An index fund SIP is the honest default: it is the return of the market, minus almost
nothing, with no stock-picking skill required. Anything you pick by hand should be
measured against it. If you can't beat it after three years, that is useful
information, not a failure.

## How I'd get this wrong

Picking last year's #1 performing fund. Top-quartile performance is the least
persistent statistic in finance; expense ratio is the most predictive one.
