# Primary vs. secondary market (where your money actually goes)

**Primary market** = the company sells new shares and keeps the cash (IPO, FPO,
rights issue). Your money reaches the business.

**Secondary market** = you buy from another investor on the exchange. The company
gets nothing. Your money goes to the person who sold. This is 99.9% of all trading.

## Simple example

Zomato IPO: you buy 100 shares at ₹76 → ₹7,600 goes to Zomato to spend on the
business. Next day you sell those 100 shares at ₹110 to Meena → ₹11,000 goes from
Meena to you. Zomato sees none of it. Its share price changed; its bank balance
didn't.

## Why this matters

- A rising price doesn't hand the company money (it does make future fundraising
  cheaper, and makes employee stock worth more).
- In an IPO you're buying from **the seller's chosen moment**. Promoters and early
  investors sell when they think the price is good — that's not automatically bad,
  but it's never an accident.
- "Oversubscribed 40x" measures demand for an allotment, not value.

## How I'd get this wrong

Thinking an IPO is cheap because it's "new". New ≠ cheap. The IPO price is set by
bankers to sell the whole book — it's usually the most optimistic price the market
will bear that week.
